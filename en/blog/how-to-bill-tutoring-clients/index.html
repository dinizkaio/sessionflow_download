How to bill tutoring clients monthly, and track it

By Kaio Diniz, creator of Session Flow · November 7, 2026

Private tutors usually bill in one of three ways: a flat monthly fee, a rate per lesson, or the month's lessons added up and billed at the end. Any of them works. The hard part is everything around it: the lesson the student missed, the makeup lesson, the holiday, the summer break, the yearly rate increase and the parent who forgot to pay.

This guide shows how to pick a billing model, what to agree on before the first lesson, how to track who has paid in a spreadsheet or an app, and how to ask for a late payment without awkwardness, with message templates.

Flat monthly fee, per lesson, or the month's lessons?

Flat monthly feePer lessonThe month's lessons added up
How it worksThe same amount every monthEach lesson has its rateLessons taught × the lesson rate
When they payOnce a monthEach lesson, or weeklyOnce a month
A missed lessonDoesn't change the amountDepends on your policyDepends on your policy
Best forRegular students, every weekOccasional lessons, test prepSchedules that change month to month

A flat monthly fee is the most predictable for both sides: you know what's coming in, and parents know what's going out. It works best when the amount is the same for four-week and five-week months. Many tutors work it out over the year (52 weeks, minus breaks) and divide by 12.

Adding up the month's lessons is fair when attendance varies, such as a student who comes twice a week before exams and once a week the rest of the term. It takes more math each month, and the amount changes from month to month.

Per lesson suits students without a fixed slot: the occasional test-prep session or conversation practice.

What to agree on before the first lesson

Almost every billing headache comes from something nobody agreed on. Before the first lesson, put it in writing, in a message to the parents or to the adult student:

  1. The rate and what it covers (lessons per week, length).
  2. When payment is due: before the month starts or after it ends, and the exact day.
  3. Missed lessons: how much notice earns a makeup, and what happens with no notice.
  4. Makeups: by when they have to happen (within the same month, for example).
  5. Breaks and holidays: whether the monthly fee changes over the summer and the winter holidays.
  6. Rate increases: when they happen (every January, for example) and how much notice you give.
  7. How to pay: Zelle, Venmo, bank transfer, cash.

Template to copy

Hi! Here's a summary of [student]'s [subject] lessons:

- Lessons: [day(s)], at [time], [length].
- Monthly fee: $[amount], due by the [day] of each month,
  via [Zelle/Venmo: details].
- Lessons cancelled at least [24 hours] ahead can be made up
  within the same month. No-shows count as taught.
- Holidays: no lesson that day, and [it's made up / the fee
  stays the same].
- Summer break: in [July], [the fee stays / is halved / is paused].
- Rate changes: in [January], with 30 days' notice.

Let me know if you have any questions.

Bill before or after the month?

Billing before (on the 1st to 10th of the month) protects you from no-shows and fits a flat monthly fee: the month is paid before the lessons happen. Billing after (on the 5th of the next month, for example) fits the month's lessons added up, because you only know the amount once the month is over.

Pick a day that works for the parents. A due date a few days after a typical payday tends to bring fewer late payments than the 1st.

No-shows, makeups and holidays

Each model handles a missed lesson differently:

  • Flat monthly fee: a no-show doesn't change the amount. A lesson cancelled with notice becomes a makeup, if that's your policy.
  • Per lesson and the month's lessons: a lesson cancelled with notice isn't charged; a no-show depends on your policy. The most common is to charge for a no-show.

A makeup is an extra lesson on another day. With a flat monthly fee, it doesn't raise the amount. When you add up the month's lessons, it counts in the month it happens: made up in another month, it moves from one month's total to the other's.

A holiday takes that day's lesson off the schedule. With a flat monthly fee, your policy says whether it's made up; when you add up the month's lessons, it simply isn't counted.

Your breaks and theirs

Summer and the winter holidays are where a flat monthly fee causes the most back-and-forth. The most common policies:

  • The fee stays the same, because the yearly amount was already divided by 12.
  • A proportional discount for the lessons that don't happen.
  • A set discount, a fixed amount for the break month.
  • No fee during the break, with the slot held for when they come back.

Whatever you choose, the discount should apply to that month only, without changing the other months.

Rate increases

One increase a year, always in the same month, is the least surprising. Give at least 30 days' notice in writing, with the new rate and the date. The old rate applies until then, including to months that haven't been paid yet.

How to track who has paid

In a spreadsheet

One row per student per month is enough:

StudentWho paysMonthAmountDuePaid onHow
Emma ClarkLaura (mom)Nov 2026320.00Nov 10Nov 8Zelle
Noah BellNoahNov 2026300.00Nov 10

When you add up the month's lessons, add a column for the number of lessons taught. Once a week, filter for an empty "Paid on" with a past due date: that's who to follow up with.

The spreadsheet starts to break down with the exceptions: a makeup from another month, the summer discount, a rate change mid-year, a group class where each student pays a different rate.

In an app

In a scheduling and payments app, you add the student once, with the time slot and how you bill, and the app does the month's math: the lessons that happened, the no-shows, the holidays and the makeups. What matters is seeing, at any moment, who has paid and what's still outstanding for each student.

How to ask for payment without awkwardness

Most late payments are forgetfulness. A short message on the due date takes care of nearly all of them.

On the due date:

Hi [name]! Just a reminder that [November]'s fee for
[student]'s lessons is due today: $[amount].
[Zelle/Venmo: details]. Thank you!

A week later:

Hi [name]! I haven't seen [November]'s payment ($[amount])
come through yet. If you've already sent it, could you share
the confirmation so I can check? [Zelle/Venmo: details].

Send it to whoever pays, usually a parent, not the student. And never bring up payment in front of the student at the end of a lesson.

Frequently asked questions

Can I charge for a lesson the student skipped without notice?

Yes, if it's in your policy from the start. The most common setup is to charge for no-shows and make up lessons cancelled with notice. Without a written policy, that conversation gets much harder.

Does a month with five weeks cost more?

With a flat monthly fee, no: the amount is the same every month, and four-week and five-week months even out over the year. When you add up the month's lessons, yes: a month with five lessons costs more.

I give siblings a discount. How do I track it?

Each student has their own rate. Add each sibling with the discounted rate, and the contact who pays can be the same for both.

What about group classes?

In a group, each student has their own rate, their own no-shows and their own payment. A student who joins mid-term starts paying from the day they join.

Can the summer fee be different?

Yes, if you agreed on it. Apply the discount to that month only, so the other months keep the usual amount.

Do I need to give parents a receipt?

When they ask, yes. A monthly receipt with the student's name, the month, the lesson dates and the amount covers most requests.
